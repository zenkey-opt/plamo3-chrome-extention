Keeping a Sourdough Starter Alive

June 12, 2026 · 6 min read

A sourdough starter is nothing more than flour and water, yet it can raise a loaf of bread without any commercial yeast. The catch is that it is alive, and like anything alive, it needs a routine.

Inside the jar, wild yeasts and lactic acid bacteria share the same food. The yeasts produce the carbon dioxide that makes dough rise, while the bacteria produce the acids that give sourdough its tang. When you feed the starter, you give both groups fresh flour to eat and dilute the acid they have built up.

A simple feeding routine

Most bakers keep a small amount of starter and refresh it once or twice a day. Discard all but about 50 grams, then mix in 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. This one-to-one ratio, known as 100 percent hydration, is easy to remember and easy to scale. If you want to try a stiffer or looser starter, our hydration calculator works out the amounts for you.

Temperature matters more than most beginners expect. At around 24 °C, a healthy starter usually doubles in four to six hours. In a cold kitchen it may take twice as long, which is not a sign that anything is wrong.

Reading the signs

  • Bubbles throughout the jar, not just on the surface, mean the yeast is active.
  • A sharp smell, a little like nail polish remover, means the starter is hungry and should be fed sooner.
  • A layer of grey liquid on top is harmless. Pour it off or stir it back in.

If you need a break from baking, keep the starter in the refrigerator and feed it once a week. Before your next bake, give it two or three feedings at room temperature until it rises predictably again. Still stuck? See Why is my starter not rising?
